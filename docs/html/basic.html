Contributor guide

Suggesting a statement or a question

You know something about how an ecosystem works, from a paper, a report, your own field notes or your community's knowledge, and you'd like it in EcoWeaver. This page tells you what to write in an issue. You don't need to know anything about schemas, files or code.

What WARP records

WARP is the structure EcoWeaver uses to store knowledge about ecological relationships. It never stores a bare fact. It stores a statement: someone, somewhere, said something, and had a reason for saying it. Every statement has three parts:

Who said ita research team, a land manager, a knowledge holder, you
What they saidone thing affects or interacts with another
What backs itan experiment, years of observation, a single season's notes

Keeping these together is what lets EcoWeaver later say things like "three experiments and two practitioners' observations support this, and one survey disputes it", without losing track of whose knowledge is whose.

Five words to know

Statement
One person's or group's claim, from one source. "Murphy and colleagues reported that planting bloodroot reduced garlic mustard growth." This is what you suggest.
Proposition
The idea a statement puts forward, separate from who said it: "planting bloodroot reduces garlic mustard growth". Many statements can share one proposition. You never need to write these: EcoWeaver groups matching statements automatically.
Open question
A proposition nobody has made a statement about yet. "Does planting bloodroot suppress garlic mustard?" You can suggest these too.
Scope
How broadly a statement is meant: about one particular place and time, or about what usually happens. It matters a lot; see picking the scope.
Evidence
What the statement rests on: an experiment, a survey, a practitioner's experience, Indigenous knowledge, a model.

Before you file

  • Is it one relationship? "A affects B" is one statement. "A affects B, which affects C" is two. A paper with five findings is five statements; list them in one issue if you like, numbered.
  • Can you point to where it comes from? A citation or DOI, a report, a dataset, or "my own field notes, spring 2025". Your own observation is a perfectly good source.
  • Is it yours, or are you allowed to share it? GitHub issues are public. Read your own and community knowledge before posting anything a community holds.
  • Is it about ecology? WARP handles cause-and-effect statements ("planting X reduces Y") and interactions between organisms ("deer eat X"). Other kinds of statement are planned; if yours doesn't fit, file it anyway and say so.

Why each line is there

LineWhy we ask
Your words and the exact wordingWe keep both. The exact wording lets anyone check that the statement wasn't stretched beyond what the source said.
SourceEvery statement must be traceable. "My notebook" is fine.
Who said itWARP credits the person or group who made the statement, separately from whoever typed it into EcoWeaver. If it's your observation, you're the author.
How broadlyStatements about one plot and statements about what generally happens are kept apart. See scope.
First thing, relationship, second thingThe core of the statement. See naming the things and relationships.
Does NOT happen"Deer don't eat garlic mustard" and "no effect was found" are real, useful results. WARP records them as statements, not as missing data.
How confidently worded"Bloodroot reduces garlic mustard" and "bloodroot may be associated with less garlic mustard" say different things. We record the hedging the source used.
What backs it upLets a synthesis weigh an experiment differently from a single observation, while still counting both.
Where / compared with whatMany ecological effects only hold in some conditions, and every effect is relative to something.
Can it be sharedWARP records consent and sharing permissions with every statement that needs them.

Naming the two things

Each statement links two things: the cause or actor, and what's affected. In WARP these are called nodes. One tip makes a big difference:

Name the thing, not the change. Write "garlic mustard population growth rate", not "decreased garlic mustard". Let the relationship carry the direction ("prevents", "causes"). That way a study finding an increase and a study finding a decrease meet on the same thing and can be compared.
Instead ofWrite
"bloodroot planting causes less garlic mustard"planting bloodroot → prevents → garlic mustard population growth rate
"burning increases native forb cover"prescribed burning → causes → native forb cover
"deer"white-tailed deer population (if the statement is about the animals)

Use scientific names if you know them (Alliaria petiolata, garlic mustard). Common names are fine too. Say what was measured if the source says: abundance, cover, growth rate, survival.

Picking the relationship

Pick the closest word. If none fits, describe the relationship in plain words and we'll match it.

Cause and effect

RelationshipUse it when the source says…
causesA produces or increases B.
contributes toA is one of several factors behind B.
enablesA makes B possible or easier, but isn't enough on its own.
preventsA reduces, blocks or inhibits B.
disruptsA degrades or suppresses B.
regulatesA changes B, but the direction depends on circumstances.
mediatesA is a step on the pathway by which something else affects B.
moderatesA changes how strongly something else affects B.
associated withA and B go together, with no claim that one causes the other.
correlated withAs above, and a correlation statistic is reported.
precedesA comes before B in time, with no causal claim.

Interactions between organisms

RelationshipUse it when…
eatsMembers of A eat B (grazing, browsing, predation).
pollinatesA pollinates B.
is a parasite ofA is a parasite of B.
interacts withA and B interact, and none of the above is more specific.

For interactions, it also helps to say whether each side benefits, is harmed, or is unaffected, if the source says.

Picking the scope

This is the one question we'll most often come back to you about, so it's worth a minute. Ask yourself: is the source talking about one particular situation, or about what happens in general?

ScopeThe statement is about…Sounds like
One casea particular place, population or event"On the plot by the path, garlic mustard declined after we planted bloodroot."
Sometimessomething that can or does happen in some cases"Bloodroot can suppress garlic mustard."
Typicallywhat usually holds, allowing exceptions"Planting bloodroot reduces garlic mustard growth." Most general ecological findings are this.
Alwaysevery case, no exceptions"Garlic mustard always…" (rare in ecology)
Not sureyou can't tell from the sourceSay so. That's useful information too.
Why it matters A single-plot observation and a general claim are kept as different propositions, even when they're about the same two things. The one-plot observation isn't thrown away: it becomes evidence for the general claim. That's how a field note can support a generalization without being mistaken for one. Statements whose scope stays unclear can't be grouped with others until someone decides.

What backs it up

Say what kind of study or knowledge the statement rests on. Pick the closest:

KindExamples
Randomized experimenttreatments randomly assigned to plots, in the field or lab
Natural experimenta wildfire, flood or policy boundary that created a comparison
Quasi-experimentbefore-after-control-impact (BACI) designs
Observation over timemonitoring plots, repeated surveys
Observation at one timea single survey comparing sites
Mechanism studywork on how the effect happens
Case studyone site or project described in detail
Practitioner experiencea land manager's or restoration practitioner's own observations
Indigenous knowledgetraditional ecological knowledge, shared with consent
Model or simulationcomputational models, theory-based predictions
Review or meta-analysisa synthesis of many studies
Expert opinionexpert elicitation, Delphi surveys

If the source reports numbers (means, sample sizes, effect sizes), mention where to find them. You don't need to copy them out.

Suggesting an open question

You can suggest a question nobody has answered yet: something a practitioner wants to know, or a gap you've noticed. It's stored as an open question, keeps its place, and is automatically marked as answered once statements about it arrive. It goes back to being open if those statements are later removed.

Your own and community knowledge

WARP treats practitioners and Indigenous knowledge holders as people making statements in their own right, not only as sources for other people's statements. If you've seen something in your own practice, you are the author of that statement, and it's recorded that way.

Indigenous and community knowledge: please don't post it in a public issue unless it's yours to share openly. GitHub issues are public and permanent. WARP records who holds a piece of knowledge, the community with authority over it, the consent it was shared under, and how it may be shared (open, with attribution, within the community only, or not beyond its original context). A statement from a knowledge holder or community member can't enter EcoWeaver without that information. If any of it applies, open an issue that only says you'd like to contribute community knowledge, and a maintainer will arrange a private route.

You can stay pseudonymous. Tell us how you'd like to be credited: a name, an ORCID, or a pseudonym.

A filled-in example

A practitioner noticed something on their site and files it:

Title: Statement: bloodroot planting and garlic mustard by the path

The statement, in your words: After bloodroot was planted by the path, there was less
  garlic mustard the following year.
Exact wording from the source: "Planted bloodroot by the path last year and noticed
  there was not as much garlic mustard this year"
Source:                       my field notebook
Who said it:                  me (credit me as "practitioner-1")
How broadly is it meant?      one case (one plot, one year)

First thing:                  planting bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis)
Relationship:                 prevents
Second thing:                 garlic mustard (Alliaria petiolata) population growth
Does it say this does NOT happen?  no
How confidently is it worded?     only an association: I noticed it, didn't test it

What backs it up:             practitioner experience
Where / when it applies:      the plot beside the path
Compared with what?          the same spot the year before
Can this be shared openly?    with attribution

A maintainer turns this into a WARP statement. Because it's about one plot, it gets its own one-case proposition. When EcoWeaver later assesses the general idea "planting bloodroot typically suppresses garlic mustard", this observation is counted as weak, supporting evidence alongside any experiments.

What happens next

  1. A maintainer records it. They'll match your two things to EcoWeaver's existing nodes (or create new ones), and may ask about scope, wording or sharing.
  2. It's checked. Automatic checks make sure every reference resolves, the right people are credited, and community knowledge carries its consent and sharing information.
  3. It's grouped. If other statements say the same thing (same two things, same relationship, same yes/no, same scope), they're grouped under one shared proposition. If yours matches an open question, the question is marked as asserted.
  4. It may feed a synthesis. EcoWeaver's syntheses weigh all the statements about a proposition and grade how certain the evidence is.

Curious how any of that works? The advanced guide explains it all, still without code.

Glossary

Node
One of the two "things" in a statement: a population, a management action, a measurable quantity.
Predicate
The relationship word: causes, prevents, eats.
Negated
A statement that something does not happen.
Hedging
How cautiously the source words it: "reduces" versus "may be associated with".
Evidence line
One argument for or against a proposition, built from evidence of one kind.
Synthesis assessment
EcoWeaver's own statement about how well the evidence supports a proposition, with a certainty grade (high, moderate, low, very low).
CARE
Principles for Indigenous data governance (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics). WARP stores consent and sharing information in this spirit.
